# What a variant is

The books of the New Testament were written in Greek in the first century. For the next fourteen hundred years, until printing, every copy was made by hand, one letter at a time. Thousands of those handwritten copies survive, whole or in pieces. They agree in the great bulk of the text. Where they do not, at one word or a few, the place is called a **variant**, and each different wording found there is a **reading**.

## A small example

In Revelation 3:1 the Lord says to the church in Sardis, in the Greek you read in Lampas:

> ὅτι ζῇς — "that you live"

Some of the Byzantine manuscripts, the large family of copies this text is drawn from, read instead:

> καὶ ζῇς — "and you live"

One small word is different, and the sense of the verse is much the same either way: the church has a name for being alive, and it is dead. That is what most variants are like.

## What the differences are

Most variants are one of these:

- **Spelling.** The same word spelled two ways, often because two letters sounded alike when the copy was made.
- **Word order.** Greek can put its words in many orders without changing what they mean, so copies differ in order often.
- **A small word.** An article ("the"), an "and", or a pronoun is there in one copy and not in another.
- **Another word.** One word stands where another stood: "this one" or "thus", "will open" or "opens".
- **Words added or left out.** Now and then a phrase, or rarely a whole verse, is in some copies and not in others.

Many variants change nothing an English reader would see: a spelling, an order, a form of a verb that English translates the same way. Others change the English by a word or two. A few, discussed in the last lesson, are longer.

## Editions and the apparatus

Nobody reads all the manuscripts at once. Scholars compare them and print an **edition**: one text, with the readings they chose. Most editions also print, under the text or in the margin, the other readings at the places that matter most to them. That list is called the **apparatus** (from the Latin for "equipment").

The Greek in Lampas is the edition of Maurice Robinson and William Pierpont, published in 2005. Their 2018 edition comes with an apparatus of two kinds: the places where the Byzantine manuscripts themselves are divided, and the places where Nestle-Aland, the modern edition most Bibles today are translated from, prints a different reading. Lampas lays that apparatus on the Greek of each chapter. There are about 7,200 such places in the New Testament, and about 5,900 of them change the English in some way, most by a word or two.

## What Lampas does with them

When you turn variants on, Lampas marks the words of each place and shows the other reading beside them. It tells you whose reading it is. It does not tell you which reading is right. Scholars who have given their lives to these questions disagree, and the next lessons set out why, and what each side holds, so that you can read the evidence yourself.
