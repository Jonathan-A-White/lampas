# When it matters

<!-- toc: none -->

With variants on, Revelation 3 alone has 29 places marked. It is fair to ask how much of this matters to a reader. The honest answer is: most of it very little, some of it a word, and a few places a great deal.

## Most change nothing you would see

Of the 7,198 places Lampas marks, about 1,300 differ only in the Greek: a spelling, the order of words, a form of a verb that English renders the same way. In Revelation 3:9 the text has ἥξωσιν, the Nestle-Aland text ἥξουσιν; both are "they will come". These matter to someone studying Greek, which is a good reason to look at them, and to almost no one else.

## Many change a word

Most of the rest change the English by a word or two. In Revelation 3:7 the text has ἀνοίξει, "will open", where Nestle-Aland has ἀνοίγει, "opens". In 1 Corinthians 1:15 the text has ἐβάπτισα, "I baptized", and Nestle-Aland ἐβαπτίσθητε, "you were baptized"; the Majority Standard Bible gives the second in a footnote. Readings like these can change how a sentence reads, and they are worth a look, but the passage says much the same either way.

## A few are long

At twenty-two places in Lampas the text's words that differ number ten or more. Among them:

- **Matthew 6:13.** The end of the Lord's Prayer, "For Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever. Amen", is in the text and not in Nestle-Aland.
- **Whole verses.** Nestle-Aland leaves out eleven whole verses that the Byzantine text has, among them John 5:4 (the angel who stirred the water of the pool), Matthew 17:21, Mark 11:26 and Romans 16:24. Bibles that follow it usually put such a verse in a footnote, which is why the verse numbers skip.

Two long passages, Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11 (the woman caught in adultery), are in both texts. Nestle-Aland prints them in double brackets, its sign for words its editors judge were added later; Robinson and Pierpont print both as part of the text. Lampas shows the brackets as they are printed.

## Weighing a place yourself

When a variant catches your eye, a few questions help.

- **Does the English change?** If not, it is a matter of Greek form.
- **Whose reading is it?** A **B** is a place where the Byzantine copies themselves divide; an **NA** or **ECM** is where a critical edition reads otherwise. The third lesson says why they differ.
- **What does each reading say in its verse?** Read the verse both ways, and the verses around it.
- **What do the editors say?** Robinson's *The Case for Byzantine Priority*, in the app from the Preface, sets out his side. For the other side, Bruce Metzger's *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* explains, place by place, why the committee behind the critical text chose its readings.
- **Ask the tutor.** From the variant's sheet, the tutor will lay out the readings and what each side holds. It will not tell you which is right.

Lampas reads the Byzantine text and says so, and shows the other readings so that nothing is hidden from you. Which reading you hold to be the original is for you to weigh, with the people who have studied it most on both sides.
