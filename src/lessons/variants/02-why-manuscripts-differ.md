# Why manuscripts differ

<!-- toc: none -->

Copying a book by hand is slow, and the people who did it were careful. Robinson and Pierpont, in the preface to their 2005 edition, call the scribes' work faithful and accurate on the whole. Even so, no two long handwritten copies agree in every letter. The preface sorts the differences into two kinds: slips that happened without anyone meaning them, and changes a scribe made on purpose.

## Slips of the eye, the ear and the hand

Most variants, the 2005 preface says, began as an ordinary mistake.

- **Letters that sound alike.** By the time most surviving copies were made, several Greek vowels were said the same way. A scribe writing from dictation, or saying the words to himself as he copied, could write ο for ω or ει for ι. Many variants are of this kind. In Revelation 3:5 the text Lampas reads has οὗτος, "this one"; the Nestle-Aland text has οὕτως, "thus": the two words differ by one vowel and an accent.
- **The eye jumps.** Two lines ending in the same letters let the eye skip from the first to the second, and the words between are lost. Scholars call this *homoioteleuton*, "like ending". Copying a word or line twice is *dittography*; writing once what should be written twice is *haplography*.
- **Words change places.** A scribe who held a phrase in his head could write it down in another order, or put a familiar word where a rarer one stood.

## Changes made on purpose

Some differences were made by a scribe who meant to make them. The 2005 preface names three: trying to restore a copy that was damaged or hard to read; smoothing a place that seemed hard in its sense, its grammar or its style; and changes made for reasons of belief. A scribe who knew a saying well from one gospel could also make another gospel's wording match it.

## Families of copies

Each copy was made from an earlier one, so a mistake, once made, passed on to every copy made from it. Over the centuries the manuscripts fell into families that share the same readings. The 2005 preface names four large groups that scholars have described: the Western, the Alexandrian, the Caesarean and the Byzantine. The last is the text of the great majority of manuscripts, the one copied in the Greek-speaking churches from at least the fourth century until printing.

## Two ways of weighing them

Here the scholars part.

Robinson and Pierpont hold that the text most manuscripts share, copied over a wide area for many centuries, is the best guide to what the apostles wrote. They argue that the other groups came from it by the slips and changes above. Robinson sets this out at length in *The Case for Byzantine Priority*, the appendix of their edition, which you can read in the app from the Preface.

Most scholars today weigh the evidence another way. They give the most weight to the oldest surviving copies, many of them from Egypt, and at each place they ask which reading best explains how the others arose. The Nestle-Aland text is made that way, place by place. Bruce Metzger and Bart Ehrman's *The Text of the New Testament*, and Kurt and Barbara Aland's book of the same name, are standard accounts of that method.

Both sides agree on most of the text and on the kinds of mistakes scribes made. They differ on how to weigh the copies, and Lampas does not settle that.
